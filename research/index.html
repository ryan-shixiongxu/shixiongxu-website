Research

I aim to go beyond state-centered studies of international organizations (IOs) and develop IO-centered research and theories. My work starts with the bureaucratic autonomy of IOs and investigates why bureaucratic officials challenge or do not challenge powerful states, and whether their perceptions of institutional legitimacy, reputation, and trust help explain these decisions.

ONGOING

Challenging Power: Autonomous Bureaucratic Action in International Human Rights Governance

This project examines why international human rights officials proactively challenge powerful states, focusing on independently initiated scrutiny and public criticism by UN human rights bodies. It investigates how bureaucratic leaders’ perceptions of institutional legitimacy shape autonomous decision-making, advancing an IO-centered understanding of international human rights governance.

COMPLETED

Self-Legitimation Through Naming and Shaming: Evidence from the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, 2006–2026

This project examines how UN human rights officials use naming and shaming to respond to cross-institutional legitimacy deficits facing the Human Rights Council, and how these statements can function as institutional self-legitimation.
